LEGOSIM · V12 RESEARCH STUDY · EXPERIMENTAL TIMING PRIOR

Human Action Timing and Temporal Shortcut Robustness

Can plausible variation in action and phase durations make an episode clock less useful for predicting what has actually happened?

We implement a bounded hierarchical timing prior and compare it with simpler controls. The study measures executed timing and a time-only state predictor. It does not establish human-distribution agreement or robustness of a trained visual policy.

Research question and decision

Human realism and shortcut resistance are separate objectives. H1: regular schedules make elapsed time predictive of state. H2: local phase variation provides information beyond an episode-wide pace factor. H3: a model should not infer new progress during a verified non-progress interval. H4: an eventual human-fitted conditional model should retain realistic geometry and pace correlations.

Recommendation: use human_timing_v1 as an opt-in experimental generator, retain the bounded Gaussian and shared-pace baselines, and select a fitted family only after compatible human event records are available. Existing nominal geometry-dependent durations set the log-location; truncation can shift medians and means. Dispersion and phase allocations are explicit assumptions. Small timing noise is not a guarantee against shortcuts.

Available human evidence

Aria reports two wearers and a pooled contact-to-release median of 2.6 s, without accessible event records, a reported event count, or untouched held-out subjects. The separate YouTube study reports 1,428 events in 18 videos, median 1.2 s and IQR 0.70–1.93 s. These populations and boundary conventions are not pooled. A median does not determine variance, tails, correlations or a conditional duration law. Frozen human-reference inventory.

Our contact frame is a simulator connector-approach proxy; the seat frame is the screened endpoint and release is the first opening frame. We do not claim that these boundaries equal anatomical human contact. Human CDF overlap, held-out likelihood and distribution-family selection are therefore not evaluated.

Before fitting, obtain event records with dataset, participant, episode, action, phase, start/end timestamps, annotation convention, censoring and supported geometry. Define reach as movement onset to first object contact, grasp as first contact to stable acquisition, carry as acquisition to destination approach, alignment as approach to first connector contact, seating as connector contact to a stable seated endpoint, and release as opening onset to loss of grasp. Search, hesitation and pauses need separate behavioral labels; a visually stationary hand alone is insufficient. Reconcile those definitions with the source annotations before comparison. Hold out participants and entire episodes, select candidate families on training/validation participants, then compare held-out log likelihood where densities are defined, CRPS or Wasserstein distance, quantiles and interval coverage. Empirical resampling has no continuous likelihood without a stated smoothing model. With two wearers and no accessible event records, that selection cannot be performed here.

Versioned implementation

log T = log nominal_duration(action, phase, geometry)
      + episode pace + action occurrence effect + phase residual
conditional distribution truncated to the allowed duration interval

The default log-space dispersions are 0.12, 0.16 and 0.10; duration multipliers are bounded to [0.7, 1.5]. Hash-keyed inverse-CDF draws use an explicit episode ID, timing seed, action occurrence and phase. These choices are assumptions, not human estimates. The Gaussian comparator uses one bounded additive multiplier per action; the shared-pace comparator shares one multiplier across authored phases.

For attachment profiles, the new request distribution uses the 2.6 s Aria median as a design anchor inside the configured contact budget (default 2–3.2 s). A 0.4–0.8 seating-fraction proposal replaces the fixed 0.64 split in the hierarchical arm, subject to nominal seating feasibility and frame rounding. Pinned total-duration bounds remain pinned. Independent action occurrences and episode IDs have separate draws.

Timing is selected before smooth motion samples are generated. Native physics samples, including free fall and driven-body receipts, retain their original clock; this version randomizes authored choreography around those intervals. It does not claim to randomize every physics-driven motion. Explicit seconds arguments remain pinned. Startup and ending padding are unchanged and remain potential timing cues. Replays check committed engine results and endpoints; sampled hand paths are not a skin/contact certificate.

Experimental design

80 of 80 requested runs completed. Five arms use the same exposed three-brick task, 16 timing trials per arm, and five small geometric offsets. The split groups by canonical scene, task, scene seed and action sequence, excluding the timing intervention and designated pause. All timing variants and repeated trials of the same underlying configuration stay together. Three base groups (9 trial IDs per arm) train the diagnostic; two base groups (7 trial IDs per arm) test it. This is a development experiment with small held-out geometry changes, not an unseen-task or human benchmark.

The label counts task pieces at their final committed render poses, using the realized frame poses and a 10⁻⁶ matrix tolerance. It is an endpoint-occupancy proxy, not a force/contact label. The time-only classifier learns one elapsed-time centroid per state from training trials. Its inputs contain no action boundaries, action IDs, labels or future clip length. Identical instructions make the instruction-plus-time comparator equivalent here. Every arm uses the same training trial IDs; native frame exposure is reported. Intervals use 1,000 cluster-bootstrap resamples of held-out base configurations. Only two independent held-out configurations are available: these descriptive intervals do not establish population-level confidence. Frames and repeated timing variants are not counted as independent base episodes.

Arm E inserts a 2–3 s explicit pause after the base has been released. Other timing draws may change when an accepted-action index changes; the contrast includes a verified non-progress interval but is not a claim that every subsequent phase has identical timing. The full cross-arm evaluation includes a held-out pause timing regime for models trained without pauses.

These are comparisons of complete timing profiles. The new B–D profiles also replace the legacy uniform attachment-budget request law, and their realized marginal variances are not matched. The shared-pace arm shares a latent quantile across contact budgets and motion, with different truncation intervals; it is not an exact global rescaling of every frame. Therefore, differences cannot be attributed uniquely to hierarchy, phase variation or pauses. The results motivate a larger, variance-matched study rather than establish an optimal distribution.

Measured findings: The legacy time-only diagnostic achieved macro F1 0.925; the separately trained hierarchical-arm diagnostic achieved 0.843. The legacy diagnostic scored 0.728 on pause episodes. These comparisons measure clock predictability, not an improvement in visual-model accuracy. No false progress was observed inside the short pause windows for this diagnostic; phantom progress was not demonstrated here. Timing errors after the pause are captured by the boundary metric. The legacy interval is degenerate because these test variants share the same deterministic timing; it is not a population-level certainty claim.

Interpretation: high time-only performance exposes useful clock information in this small task. Changed performance under timing interventions shows sensitivity of this diagnostic. It does not demonstrate that a supplied visual policy uses that information or that randomization removes the shortcut. Full visual inference, visual ablations, event-based memory, repeated-event counting and real-human transfer remain unrun.

Downstream experiments to run

Audit the actual adapter before training. Current exported causal prefixes start at frame zero and include observation end time, frame indices, FPS and timed messages. Removing timestamps alone leaves prefix length and potentially memory slots or positional encodings. Keep decoding metadata separate from learned features, prohibit future frames and privileged phase labels, and retain necessary causal history when testing sampling changes.

Compare full visual history, no-vision and visual-ablation conditions on matched timing interventions and real held-out participants. Test false progress during pauses, genuine repeated actions returning to the same state, and legitimate time-sensitive tasks. Any deployable event-indexed memory must derive events from available observations; simulator events are a diagnostic oracle only. The included scorer accepts separately produced predictions, reports missing episode coverage, and never manufactures model results.

Primary references and scope

  1. Uncovering Hidden Challenges in Query-Based Video Moment Retrieval — Mayu Otani, Yuta Nakashima, Esa Rahtu, Janne Heikkila (2020), BMVC; peer-reviewed. Blind baselines exploit temporal-position and query priors; some models respond weakly to randomized visual inputs. Include a no-vision baseline and targeted visual/timing interventions. Scope: Temporal sentence grounding, not LegoSim state recognition or robotics.
  2. Is Your Robot's Memory Just a Clock? Temporal Shortcuts in Memory-Augmented VLA Policies — Chubin Zhang, Bo An (2026), arXiv:2611.14488, submitted 10 November 2026; preprint. On tested counting tasks, null intervals cause phantom progress; removing timestamps leaves implicit slot-count cues. Pause augmentation does not cure the failure; event-indexed memory helps. Test false progress during verified pauses and audit memory indexing separately from timestamps. Scope: Limited policy/task scope. The event detector uses simulator information and is diagnostic, not a demonstrated deployable visual event detector.
  3. Computational State Space Models for Activity and Intention Recognition. A Feasibility Study — Frank Kruger, Martin Nyolt, Kristina Yordanova, Albert Hein, Thomas Kirste (2014), PLOS ONE 9(11):e109381; peer-reviewed. Section 3.1.3 compares duration models; different action classes favor Weibull, Gamma or lognormal distributions. Compare duration families on event-level held-out observations; do not declare a universal Gaussian or lognormal law. Scope: Everyday activity and inertial-sensor setting; does not provide LEGO timing parameters.
  4. The Information Capacity of the Human Motor System in Controlling the Amplitude of Movement — Paul M. Fitts (1954), Journal of Experimental Psychology 47:381-391; peer-reviewed; linked reprint. Aimed movement duration depends on movement amplitude and target tolerance. Retain geometry-dependent timing and seek precision-conditioned annotations. Scope: Does not specify the residual distribution or all assembly/search/dwell phases.
  5. Causal Confusion in Imitation Learning — Pim de Haan, Dinesh Jayaraman, Sergey Levine (2019), NeurIPS; peer-reviewed. Imitation learning can exploit non-causal correlations and fail under distribution shift; targeted interventions help identify relevant causes. Use matched timing interventions rather than relying only on in-distribution accuracy. Scope: Not direct evidence that this simulator's downstream model has a timing shortcut.
  6. SpeedNet: Learning the Speediness in Videos — Sagie Benaim et al. (2020), CVPR; peer-reviewed. Learning natural versus sped-up video provides useful motion representations. Preserve legitimate motion and speed information while testing inappropriate progress-from-clock inference. Scope: Does not establish that speed augmentation eliminates temporal shortcuts.
